Applied groundwater recharge planning

From Mapping
to Decision

How spatial evidence, field judgement, construction learning and groundwater monitoring shaped an applied recharge-planning approach.

Kaushal GadariyaSoil and Water Conservation Engineer
120interventions
109unique sites
19lithologs

Groundwater-recharge planning often begins with a spatial question: where does the landscape appear favourable for recharge? In practice, that question soon opens into several others. What water is actually available at a site? What hydrological function can the location support? What does construction reveal below the ground? And what should monitoring change in the next round of planning?

This article follows that progression through my own professional learning. The work began with basin-scale spatial screening during postgraduate research and later moved into applied groundwater-recharge planning, field verification, engineering implementation, litholog interpretation, hydrogeological cross-sections and monitoring. The thread connecting these stages is simple: a map can organise evidence; the decision grows stronger as more kinds of evidence are brought together.

Where the enquiry began

In 2021, my M.Tech thesis, Identification of Sites for Groundwater Recharge Structures by Mapping of Lineaments and Stream Networks, examined groundwater-recharge planning in the Panam Basin of Gujarat. The study used remote sensing, GIS and multi-criteria decision analysis with AHP-based weighting to combine six thematic inputs: lineament density, stream density, runoff, slope, land use/land cover and soil.

The thesis model identified 201 candidate locations under that historical framework: 34 check dams, 28 percolation tanks and 139 nala bunds. More important for my later practice was the logic behind the exercise. The research treated spatial analysis as a way to screen a large landscape systematically, then carried site selection into structure-specific criteria and physical ground truthing.

The thesis also recognised that mapped recharge-potential areas required further hydrometeorological, hydrological and hydrogeological investigation. That point became more important to me with field experience. Spatial screening was useful because it made a large landscape legible. The next professional question was how to turn that spatial hypothesis into a location-specific decision.

Reader takeaway: Spatial analysis can narrow a landscape into questions worth investigating.

Reading the landscape as a spatial hypothesis

The applied Groundwater Recharge Potential Zonation approach used in this work brings together five factors: Aquifer, Slope, Drainage Density, Lineament Density and LULC. Their weights are 30%, 20%, 20%, 15% and 15% respectively. Together they provide a structured view of terrain, surface-water organisation, land cover and subsurface context.

Each layer answers a different planning question. Aquifer mapping provides regional subsurface context. Slope helps read the balance between runoff and opportunity for detention or infiltration. Drainage density describes how the landscape organises surface flow. Lineament density contributes structural context. LULC helps distinguish settings such as agriculture, habitat, river bed, shrubs and waterbodies.

The final GWRPZ class layer used in this article is the supplied authoritative GWRPZ_Class.tif. I use it as a decision-support layer: a way to organise recharge-potential evidence before field and engineering information are added.

Method note: The surviving evidence fully supports the five-factor weighting and the supplied final class raster. The exact historical score-to-final-class break rule is only partly recoverable, so the supplied class raster remains the publication reference.

What the implemented sites taught us

The implementation evidence became the first major reality check on the map. The original intervention inventory contains 122 records. Two records have unresolved cross-field intervention-label conflicts, so the publication analysis uses a defensible cohort of 120 interventions at 109 unique sites.

When those 120 interventions are sampled against the authoritative final GWRPZ layer, 52 fall in High recharge-potential areas and 68 in Moderate areas. None of the current publication cohort falls in Low. The larger share in Moderate areas was important because it showed how implementation was already drawing on a wider evidence set than recharge class alone.

Drainage context adds another part of that picture. Of the 120 interventions, 81 are nearest to Order 1–2 mapped drainage and 96 are nearest to Order 1–3. The median nearest-drainage distance is 33.6 m, and 90 interventions lie within 100 m of mapped drainage. This supports a strong lower-order drainage tendency across the portfolio while still leaving room for intervention types whose logic is tied to ponds, roofs or other site conditions.

Mapped lineaments are useful in a different way. The median nearest-lineament distance across the cohort is 855.3 m, with 24 of 120 interventions within 250 m. I therefore use lineaments as structural investigation context—something that can guide questions in the field and subsurface analysis.

The map was becoming more useful when I stopped asking only, “Is this a high-potential location?” and started asking, “What opportunity does this site actually offer?”

Read the opportunity before choosing the intervention

That shift—from class to opportunity—is the centre of the applied approach. A site can be spatially promising in more than one way. One location may offer ponded runoff that can be routed downward. Another may sit along a drainage corridor where detention can be increased. A third may contain an existing pond whose storage can be enhanced. In a settlement setting, a roof and an existing dry open well can create a different recharge pathway.

For Recharge Borewells, the field logic focused on community ponds with available runoff or regular overflow, identified through GIS, field observation and community consultation. In the current cohort, 45 of 54 Recharge Borewell interventions are nearest to Order 1–2 drainage, 27 are inside mapped Waterbodies and 31 are within 30 m of mapped Waterbodies. The mapped evidence supports the pond-and-runoff context; field observation and community knowledge establish how water actually behaves at a specific pond.

For Pond Deepening/Widening, the hydrological purpose is to increase capture volume, detention and recharge opportunity at an existing storage point. Eleven of 13 interventions lie within 30 m of mapped Waterbodies, and all 13 are nearest to Order 1–2 drainage. The pattern is consistent with an intervention that works with an existing surface-water feature.

For Checkdam/Nala Deepening-Widening, drainage position becomes central. The median nearest-drainage distance is 18.5 m, and 36 of 41 interventions fall within 100 m of mapped drainage. New Checkdams show a similar channel-oriented logic: the median nearest-drainage distance is 12.4 m, and 4 of 5 are within 100 m.

Rooftop Rainwater Harvesting Systems (RRWHS) reflect a different opportunity. Field selection used large roof catchments together with an existing dry open well. Four of the five RRWHS interventions fall inside mapped Habitat. Here the mapped settlement context is useful, while roof area and well condition come from site investigation and design.

Seen together, these patterns support intervention matching. GWRPZ, drainage, waterbody, habitat, slope, aquifer and lineament context each contribute something, but their decision role changes with the intervention. Field observation, community consultation and engineering feasibility complete the picture.

Reader takeaway: The practical question is what function the site can support with the water and conditions that are present.

When construction starts revealing the subsurface

Once implementation begins, the work can generate evidence that surface mapping cannot see directly. The primary hydrogeological dataset contains 19 lithologs linked to implemented Recharge Borewell sites. Drilling represented in the dataset took place between 6 and 17 June 2025, with recorded depths from 30.00 to 51.90 m. The mean depth is 43.69 m and the median is 47.24 m.

The logs record layered sequences that include Clay Soil, Clay shales, Gravel and Conglomerate, Variegated Sandstone, Claystone and occasional Chert. The exact sequence differs from site to site. That variation is itself useful: the subsurface begins to move from a regional polygon or modelled context into direct evidence from the implemented location.

All 19 litholog locations link to intervention sites that include a Recharge Borewell. This creates a strong bridge between planning and construction. The map describes the surface and regional context; the borehole records what was encountered vertically at the site.

Some intervals in the primary records carry a source flag Y. In the public visual, that flag is retained exactly as recorded. It is a field in the source litholog record and is kept separate from any interpretation of quantified aquifer yield.

For me, this was an important change in the working process: implementation itself had become a source of hydrogeological learning.

Connecting the points below ground

A borehole gives direct evidence at one point. The next question is how several points can be read together. In the cross-section workflow, litholog depths are converted to elevations and viewed alongside drainage, lineament, watershed, DEM and geological context.

The primary package contains six conceptual cross-section traces, labelled A–F. For the representative A–AA section, reconciliation with the primary litholog dataset gives six source logs. Their collar elevations and logged depths can be placed along the retained conceptual chainage to compare vertical sequences in a common spatial frame.

The role of the section is interpretive. Nearby boreholes can be projected onto a conceptual trace, so the public figure keeps each borehole sequence discrete and uses the section line to organise their spatial relationship. This helps examine how logged units vary laterally and vertically while preserving the distinction between measured borehole evidence and interpretation between points.

Reader takeaway: The planning question deepens from where recharge appears favourable to how the receiving formations are arranged below ground.

Monitoring keeps the question open

Subsurface evidence adds depth to the site picture. Monitoring adds time. The recovered groundwater dataset contains authoritative class rasters for June 2024, June 2025 and June 2026 on the same grid, using six groundwater-depth classes: 0–2, 2–5, 5–10, 10–20, 20–40 and >40 m below ground level.

At the 109 defensible intervention sites, the 10–20 m bgl class becomes dominant in 2025 and 2026, containing 81 sites in 2025 and 89 sites in 2026. Looking at class movement, 45 sites shift to a shallower class from 2024 to 2025, 61 stay in the same class and 3 shift deeper. From 2025 to 2026, the pattern is 14 shallower, 81 same and 14 deeper. Across 2024 to 2026, it is 46 shallower, 56 same and 7 deeper.

I use these transitions as monitoring feedback. They show how groundwater-depth classes vary across time at the intervention-site locations and help frame the next questions for planning. Attribution would require additional evidence on rainfall, pumping, well lineage and timing, so the article keeps the monitoring layer descriptive.

Monitoring therefore closes the loop and keeps the analysis open. It gives the next planning cycle a new evidence layer.

What changed in the way recharge is planned

Looking back across the work, the biggest change has been in the question itself. My postgraduate research asked how a large basin could be screened systematically. Applied work pushed the question toward site function: what water is available here, what intervention fits, and what must be checked in the field? Construction added another question—what is actually below this location? Monitoring then asks what the next decision should learn from time.

The working approach can now be summarised as four connected actions:

Map → Verify → Understand → Monitor

  • Map — organise spatial evidence and identify questions worth investigating.
  • Verify — read runoff, site function and engineering feasibility with people in the field.
  • Understand — use construction and lithologs to deepen the subsurface picture.
  • Monitor — carry temporal evidence into the next planning decision.

“GIS helps validate the evidence. The decision still has to be made in the field, with the community.”

For me, that sentence now captures the role of GIS more accurately than a single suitability score ever could. The map remains important because it makes patterns visible and comparable. Field evidence gives those patterns meaning. Construction can generate new subsurface knowledge. Monitoring keeps the process open to revision.

How the evidence was assembled

Spatial evidence

The current GWRPZ framework uses five factors—Aquifer, Slope, Drainage Density, Lineament Density and LULC—and the supplied final GWRPZ class raster is used as the authoritative publication layer. The implementation analysis begins with 122 source records; two unresolved cross-field label conflicts are retained internally and excluded from publication analytics, leaving 120 interventions at 109 unique sites.

Field and intervention evidence

Intervention-selection logic is drawn from practitioner-confirmed field process, including diagnosis of existing water-harvesting structures, runoff and overflow observation, community consultation and engineering feasibility. Spatial relationships by intervention type are used as supporting evidence for those field functions.

Subsurface evidence

Nineteen primary lithologs are linked directly to implemented Recharge Borewell sites. The public litholog explorer uses anonymous IDs. Cross-section interpretation uses reconciled primary logs together with the documented depth-to-elevation and conceptual-profile method.

Monitoring evidence

Recovered groundwater-class rasters for June 2024, June 2025 and June 2026 provide a common class-based temporal layer. Site-level class transitions are treated as planning feedback.

Evidence boundaries

Surface GIS is used to organise and test spatial hypotheses. Site investigation remains essential for runoff behaviour, structure condition and feasibility. The litholog subset represents implemented Recharge Borewell sites. Source flag Y is retained as a source flag. Groundwater class transitions are used as temporal context for the next planning decision; no intervention-effect estimate is derived from them.
